# Roadmap: highest weight theory, part II — sl₂-triples, weight filtrations and weight patterns

**Summary.** Attach to every `sl₂`-triple of a Lie algebra, the zero triple included, an increasing filtration on each finite-dimensional module, and prove that the dimensions of these filtrations, taken over all modules, determine the triple up to inner automorphism. In the classical types the standard module suffices except for very even partitions in type `D`, where one half-spin module separates the two classes; in the exceptional types the adjoint module suffices, by a finite comparison of root profiles carried out directly in each of `G₂`, `F₄`, `E₆`, `E₇`, `E₈`. A second strand proves the highest-weight half-integrality test and shows that the geometric weight patterns of a semisimple element of a complex semisimple group determine its positive real part up to conjugacy. This continues LieHighestWeight, which stops at ordinary highest weight theory of semisimple Lie algebras (`sl₂` theory, weights, Verma modules, classification of irreducibles, Weyl's theorem, characters, the centre and the Serre presentation) and does not treat `sl₂`-triples of a general semisimple algebra, their filtrations or their conjugacy. The end theorems are those used by Ciubotaru–Harris (Lemmas 2.4, 6.30, 10.9 and Proposition 10.2): filtration recognition over a semisimple Lie algebra, its classical and exceptional refinements, and recognition of the positive real part.

## Conventions

`K` is an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, except in §§5.3–5.5, where the field is `ℂ`. `L` is a Lie algebra over `K`; it is finite-dimensional with nondegenerate Killing form (`LieAlgebra.IsKilling`) wherever conjugacy is discussed, which over `K` is semisimplicity. Modules are Lie modules over `K`; "representation" means a finite-dimensional Lie module unless an algebraic representation of a group is named. Half-spin modules are Lie modules of `so(2r)`; nothing here asks them to descend to a special orthogonal group.

A triple is an ordered `(h, e, f)` with `[e, f] = h`, `[h, e] = 2e`, `[h, f] = −2f`; `h` is its neutral element and `e` its nilpositive element. The zero triple `(0, 0, 0)` is a triple in this sense. Mathlib's `IsSl2Triple` adds `h ≠ 0`; in characteristic zero the two notions differ exactly by the zero triple (§0.1).

Conjugacy of triples means conjugacy under `Int(L)`, the subgroup of linear automorphisms of `L` generated by `exp(ad x)` for ad-nilpotent `x ∈ L` (LieHighestWeight Layer 7). For a connected semisimple algebraic group `G` over `K` with a fixed isomorphism `Lie(G) ≅ L`, it is conjugacy under `Ad(G(K))` (§0.2).

For a triple `t = (h, e, f)` and a module `M` write `M_j = ker(ρ(h) − j)` for `j ∈ ℤ` and `W_i(M) = Σ_(j ≤ i) M_j`. The filtration is increasing and `e` raises the index by two: `ρ(e) W_i ⊆ W_(i+2)`. Write `d_i = dim W_i − dim W_(i−1) = dim M_i` (the graded weight multiplicities) and, for `n ≥ 0`, `m_n = d_n − d_(n+2)` (the multiplicity of the standard module `V(n)` of dimension `n + 1`). Three integer sequences are in play — the cumulative dimensions `dim W_i`, the graded multiplicities `d_i` and the `sl₂` multiplicities `m_n` — and they are never identified: for the adjoint module of `sl₂` they are `1, 1, 2, 2, 3` (at `i = −2, …, 2`), `1, 0, 1, 0, 1` and `m_2 = 1`. Each `W_i` is a subspace, generally not an `L`-submodule. Reindexing `Fil^n = W_(−n)` gives a decreasing filtration; the ordering used throughout is the increasing one of Ciubotaru–Harris (CH Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 288).

Exceptional root systems use Bourbaki numbering with Cartan entries `A_ij = ⟨α_i, α_j∨⟩`, as pinned by `TauCeti.DynkinType.cartanMatrix`, and roots are written in coordinates on the simple roots. `G₂` has `A = [[2, −1], [−3, 2]]` (`α₁` short); `F₄` has `A₂₃ = −2`, `A₃₂ = −1` (`α₁, α₂` long); the `E_r` edges are `(1,3), (3,4), (2,4), (4,5), …, (r−1, r)`. The opposition involution `−w₀` exchanges `1 ↔ 6` and `3 ↔ 5` in `E₆` and is trivial for `G₂, F₄, E₇, E₈`. Mathlib's `CartanMatrix.G₂` is the transpose of this matrix; the isomorphism class of `Matrix.ToLieAlgebra` does not depend on the choice.

For `so(2r)` use coordinates `ε₁, …, ε_r` on a Cartan subalgebra, simple roots `ε_i − ε_(i+1)` (`i < r`) and `ε_(r−1) + ε_r`, so that the dominant chamber is `a₁ ≥ ⋯ ≥ a_(r−1) ≥ |a_r|` and `ω_r = ½(ε₁ + ⋯ + ε_r)`; this is the convention of SpinRepresentations Layer 5. The half-spin module `L(ω_r)` has as weights the vectors `½(±1, …, ±1)` with an even number of minus signs.

In Layer 5, `q > 1` is a fixed real number and absolute values are the usual ones on `ℂ`. A semisimple element `s` of a connected complex semisimple group is `q`-geometric if in every finite-dimensional algebraic representation every eigenvalue of `s` has absolute value `q^(i/2)` for some `i ∈ ℤ` (the complex form of CH Definition 2.5, p.7, CH line 323). Real functionals in §§5.1–5.2 act on the abstract real weight space `X ⊗ ℝ`; no ordering of `K` is used.

The quantifier over modules in the recognition theorem ranges over a universe containing `K` and `L`, so that the adjoint module and every `Kⁿ` occur.

## How to read the build

Layer 0 fixes the objects: zero-inclusive triples, the inner automorphism group and its comparison with the adjoint group, invariance of modules under inner twists, and conjugacy of triples from their neutral elements. Layer 1 builds the weight filtration of a triple on every module and proves that its dimensions are exactly the data of the restricted `sl₂`-module, with exactness and tensor and dual laws. ## Layer 0: zero-inclusive triples and inner conjugacy

The zero triple is part of every statement: it is the triple of the zero nilpotent element, and its filtration is concentrated in degree zero (CH §2.1, p.6, CH line 277).

### 0.1 Zero-inclusive triples

Define `IsSl2TripleOrZero h e f` as the conjunction of `[e, f] = h`, `[h, e] = 2e` and `[h, f] = −2f`, with natural-number scalars as in `IsSl2Triple`, and no condition on `h`. Over a field of characteristic zero, `IsSl2TripleOrZero h e f` holds if and only if `(h, e, f) = (0, 0, 0)` or `IsSl2Triple h e f`: if `h = 0` then `2e = 0` and `2f = 0`, so `e = f = 0` because `2` is invertible (CH §2.1, relations (2.1), p.6, CH line 279).

API:

- The zero triple satisfies the predicate; a triple with `h ≠ 0` satisfies it exactly when it satisfies `IsSl2Triple`.
- In characteristic zero, `h = 0` forces `e = f = 0`, and `e = 0` forces `h = 0` and then `f = 0`.
- A Lie algebra homomorphism `φ` carries zero-inclusive triples to zero-inclusive triples, `(φh, φe, φf)`; identity and composition act as expected. A non-injective `φ` can carry a nonzero triple to the zero triple.
- Negation and exchange: `(−h, f, e)` is a zero-inclusive triple whenever `(h, e, f)` is.
- A zero-inclusive triple determines, through `TauCeti.lieHomOfSl2Basis`, a Lie homomorphism `sl(2, K) → L` sending the standard basis to `(e, f, h)`; it is zero exactly for the zero triple.

**Checks.**

- `(0, 0, 0)` satisfies the predicate but not `IsSl2Triple`, whose field `h ≠ 0` fails; a definition that only renamed `IsSl2Triple` would exclude the zero nilpotent element.
- In `sl(2, K)` the standard `H = diag(1, −1)`, `E = E₁₂`, `F = E₂₁` satisfy it: `[E, F] = H`, `[H, E] = 2E`, `[H, F] = −2F`.
- `(0, E, 0)` fails: `[0, E] = 0 ≠ 2E`. A central nonzero `h` with `e = f = 0` fails `[e, f] = h`. Exchanging `E` and `F` while keeping `H` fails `[F, E] = −H ≠ H`; `(−H, F, E)` satisfies it.
- Over a ring with `2 = 0`, `(0, e, f)` with arbitrary `e, f` satisfies the three relations; the characteristic-zero hypothesis is what makes the zero branch a single triple.

*Needs:* Mathlib `IsSl2Triple`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.lieHomOfSl2Basis`.

### 0.2 Inner automorphisms, the adjoint group and inner twists

Let `G` be a connected semisimple algebraic group over `K` with an isomorphism `Lie(G) ≅ L`. Prove that the image of `Ad : G(K) → Aut(L)` equals `Int(L)`. For an ad-nilpotent `x ∈ L`, `x` acts nilpotently in a faithful representation `G ↪ GL(V)` (`TauCeti.isNilpotent_toEnd_of_isNilpotent_ad`), the finite exponential `exp(tx)` preserves the defining Hopf ideal of `G` (the invariant-derivation argument of ReductiveGroups Layer 5P), and differentiating the adjoint action along this one-parameter subgroup (ReductiveGroups Layer 2) gives `Ad(exp x) = exp(ad x)`; so `Int(L) ⊆ Ad(G(K))`; conversely `G(K)` is generated by its root subgroups `U_α(K) = exp(K e_α)` (ReductiveGroups Layer 7), whose adjoint images are the generators `exp(t ad e_α)` of `Int(L)`. The image does not depend on the isogeny class of `G`, because a central isogeny is surjective on `K`-points and has central kernel (ReductiveGroups Layer 6).

Prove inner invariance of modules: for every finite-dimensional `L`-module `M`, with `L` finite-dimensional Killing, and every `φ ∈ Int(L)`, the twisted module `M^φ` (action `x · m = φ(x)m`) is isomorphic to `M`. For a generator `φ = exp(ad x)`, `ρ(x)` is nilpotent (`TauCeti.isNilpotent_toEnd_of_isNilpotent_ad`) and `exp(ρ(x))` intertwines: `exp(ρ x) ρ(y) exp(−ρ x) = ρ(exp(ad x) y)`, by `TauCeti.expAd_apply_eq_exp_mul_exp_neg` in `End(M)` after applying `ρ` to `exp(ad x)y`. Products of intertwiners handle products of generators. This holds for every module, including those that do not integrate to the chosen `G` (CH Appendix, Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1703, quantifies over all modules of the Lie algebra).

API:

- `Int(L)` consists of Lie automorphisms; it is generated by `exp(ad x)` for `x` ad-nilpotent, and `exp(ad x)⁻¹ = exp(ad(−x))`.
- For a triple `(h, e, f)` with `e, f` ad-nilpotent, `TauCeti.weylAut` lies in `Int(L)` and sends `h` to `−h`; so `h` and `−h` are `Int(L)`-conjugate.
- Inner invariance: for `φ ∈ Int(L)` and a triple `t`, the filtrations of `t` and of `φ(t)` on `M` have the same dimensions in every degree.
- For a connected semisimple `G` with `Lie(G) ≅ L`, `Ad(G(K))`-conjugacy of triples is `Int(L)`-conjugacy.

**Checks.**

- For `L = sl(2, K)` and `G = SL₂`, `Ad(diag(c, c⁻¹))` acts on `E` by `c²`. With `x(t) = exp(tE)`, `y(t) = exp(tF)` and `w(c) = x(c) y(−c⁻¹) x(c) = [[0, c], [−c⁻¹, 0]]`, one has `diag(c, c⁻¹) = w(c) w(1)⁻¹`, so this automorphism is a product of generators `exp(t ad E)`, `exp(t ad F)` of `Int(sl₂)`; `Ad(−I)` is the identity, matching `Ad(SL₂(K)) = PGL₂(K) = Int(sl₂)`.
- For an abelian `L` (not Killing) every element is ad-nilpotent with `exp(ad x) = id`, so `Int(L)` is trivial, while `exp(ρ(x))` need not exist; the Killing hypothesis in inner invariance is what makes `ρ(x)` nilpotent.
- For `so(8)` and `G = SO(8)`, the half-spin module `L(ω₄)` does not descend to `SO(8)`, yet inner invariance applies to it: `exp(ρ(x))` is defined from the Lie action alone.
- An outer automorphism need not preserve filtration dimensions on a fixed module: the automorphism of `so(8)` induced by a reflection in `O(8)` exchanges `L(ω₃)` and `L(ω₄)`.

*Needs:* LieHighestWeight Layer 7 (`Int(L)`); ReductiveGroups Layers 5P, 6, 7; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.expAd`, `TauCeti.expAd_apply_eq_exp_mul_exp_neg`, `TauCeti.isNilpotent_toEnd_of_isNilpotent_ad`, `TauCeti.weylAut`.

### 0.3 Triple conjugacy from the neutral element

Let `L` be finite-dimensional Killing over `K`. Two zero-inclusive triples `(h, e, f)` and `(h', e', f')` are `Int(L)`-conjugate if and only if `h` and `h'` are `Int(L)`-conjugate. The zero triple is conjugate only to itself. Consequently a triple is determined up to conjugacy by the dominant representative of its neutral element in a fixed Cartan subalgebra (§3.1), its characteristic.

A proof can proceed as follows. Reduce to `h = h'` (the zero case is immediate since `h = 0` forces `e = f = 0`). Let `L_j` be the `ad h`-eigenspaces; by NilpotentOrbits N2.2, `[L_0, e] = L_2`. Let `G` be the adjoint group of `L` over `K` (a connected semisimple group with `Lie(G) ≅ L`, ReductiveGroups Layer 6) and `G_h` the centralizer of `h`, with `Lie(G_h) = L_0` (ReductiveGroups Layers 2–3); the orbit map `G_h → L_2`, `g ↦ Ad(g)e`, has surjective differential `u ↦ [u, e]` at the identity, so in characteristic zero its image is open and dense in `L_2` (DG §3, Lemma 3, p.4, DG line 143). The same holds for `e'`; two dense open orbits in the irreducible `L_2` meet, so `Ad(g)e = e'` for some `g ∈ G_h(K)`, and `Ad(g) ∈ Int(L)` by §0.2. Finally `f` is determined by `h` and `e`: two choices differ by an element of `ker(ad e) ∩ L_(−2)`, which is zero by `sl₂` theory. (DG §3, Lemmas 2–3, p.4, DG line 139; SOM §3.1, pp.15–16, SOM line 364.)

**Checks.**

- In `sl(2, K)` the dominant neutral elements are `0` and `H`; they give the two classes, the zero triple and the standard triple.
- In `sl(3, K)` the neutral elements `diag(1, 0, −1)` and `diag(2, 0, −2)` belong to the minimal and the regular nilpotent classes; they are not conjugate (their eigenvalue sets differ), and neither are the triples.
- In `so(8)` the two very even classes of partition `(4, 4)` are exchanged by the automorphism induced by an element of `O(8)` of determinant `−1`; their neutral elements are not `Int(so(8))`-conjugate, and the theorem keeps the two classes apart (§2.3). Replacing `Int(L)` by the full automorphism group would merge them.
- Equality of `h` alone is necessary but `e` is not determined by `h` as an element: in `sl(2, K)`, `(H, cE, c⁻¹F)` is a triple for every `c ≠ 0`, and these are conjugate by `Ad(diag(c^(1/2), c^(−1/2)))`.

*Needs:* §0.1; §0.2; NilpotentOrbits N2.2 (`[𝔤(0), e] = 𝔤(2)`); ReductiveGroups Layers 2, 3, 6; LieHighestWeight Layer 0.

### Examples

In `sl(2, K)` there are exactly two classes of zero-inclusive triples, represented by `(0, 0, 0)` and `(H, E, F)`. The Weyl automorphism `exp(ad E) exp(−ad F) exp(ad E)` sends `(H, E, F)` to `(−H, −F, −E)`, a triple conjugate to the standard one, and the neutral element `−H` is conjugate to `H`.

## Layer 1: representation filtrations and graded multiplicities

### 1.1 Restriction along a triple

A zero-inclusive triple `t = (h, e, f)` in `L` gives the Lie homomorphism `sl(2, K) → L` of §0.1; pulling a module `M` back along it gives the restricted `sl₂`-module `M|_t`, on which `H, E, F` act by `ρ(h), ρ(e), ρ(f)`. For the zero triple every element of `sl₂` acts by zero (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1707).

API:

- The restricted actions of `H, E, F` are `ρ(h), ρ(e), ρ(f)`; restriction is the pullback of Lie modules along a homomorphism, not a second module carrier.
- An `L`-module map is an `sl₂`-module map between the restrictions; identities and composition are preserved.
- Along the identity of `sl(2, K)` with its standard triple, restriction is the identity.
- For the zero triple the restriction is a direct sum of `dim M` trivial modules.

**Checks.**

- A two-dimensional trivial `L`-module restricts, for any triple, to two copies of the trivial `sl₂`-module.
- The standard module `K²` of `sl(2, K)` restricts along the standard triple to `V(1)`, with `H`-weights `1` and `−1`; along `(−H, F, E)` it restricts to a module isomorphic to `V(1)` by the coordinate swap.
- The adjoint module of `sl(3, K)` restricted along the minimal triple (`h = diag(1, 0, −1)`) has `H`-weights `2, 1, 1, 0, 0, −1, −1, −2`, so it is `V(2) ⊕ 2V(1) ⊕ V(0)`; restricted along the zero triple it is `8V(0)`.

*Needs:* §0.1; Mathlib `LieModule.toEnd`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.lieHomOfSl2Basis`.

### 1.2 The increasing weight filtration

Define `weightFiltration K h M i` as the sum over the integers `j ≤ i` of the eigenspaces `ker(ρ(h) − j)`, a subspace of `M` (CH §2.2, Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 288). For a zero-inclusive triple on a finite-dimensional module over `K` the family is increasing, zero for `i` sufficiently negative and equal to `M` for `i` sufficiently positive; with the inclusions it is an object of `CategoryTheory.FilteredObject` indexed by `ℤ` on the underlying module `M`.

API:

- For `i ≤ k`, the `i`-th step is contained in the `k`-th; for the neutral element of a zero-inclusive triple and finite-dimensional `M`, the step is `0` for `i < −N` and `M` for `i ≥ N`, where `N` is the largest absolute value of an eigenvalue.
- The successive quotient at `i` is the eigenspace `M_i` (§1.3); the eigenspaces at non-integers are zero (`TauCeti.exists_int_of_hasEigenvalue` for `h ≠ 0`, directly for `h = 0`).
- `ρ(e)` maps the `i`-th step into the `(i+2)`-th and `ρ(f)` maps it into the `(i−2)`-th; `ρ(h)` preserves every step and acts by `i` on the `i`-th successive quotient (the defining properties of an `SL(2)`-type filtration, CH Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 291).
- Every `L`-module map `M → M'` sends the `i`-th step of `M` into the `i`-th step of `M'`; identities and composition are preserved.
- For the zero triple the step is `0` for `i < 0` and `M` for `i ≥ 0`.
- For `φ ∈ Int(L)` the filtrations of `φ(t)` and `t` on `M` have equal dimensions (§0.2).

**Checks.**

- For the one-dimensional trivial module the only jump is at `0`, whatever the triple.
- For `V(1)` along the standard triple, `dim W_i` is `0` for `i < −1`, `1` for `−1 ≤ i < 1` and `2` for `i ≥ 1`.
- For the adjoint module of `sl₂`, `dim W_(−2) = 1`, `dim W_(−1) = 1`, `dim W_0 = 2`, `dim W_1 = 2`, `dim W_2 = 3`; the graded multiplicities are `1, 0, 1, 0, 1`. A definition by the decreasing convention `Fil^i = Σ_(j ≥ i) M_j` gives `3, 2, 2, 1, 1` instead.
- Each step is generally not a submodule: in the adjoint module of `sl₂`, `W_(−2) = K F` is not stable under `ad E`.

*Needs:* §0.1; §1.1; Mathlib `Module.End.eigenspace`, `CategoryTheory.FilteredObject`; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.exists_int_of_hasEigenvalue`, `TauCeti.iSup_eigenspace_toEnd_intCast_eq_top`.

### 1.3 Graded pieces and recovery of the restricted sl₂-module

Let `t` be a zero-inclusive triple and `M` finite-dimensional. The inclusion of `M_i` into the `i`-th step induces an isomorphism `M_i ≅ W_i / W_(i−1)`. Hence `d_i = dim M_i`, `d_i = d_(−i)`, `d_i` has finite support, and for `n ≥ 0` the multiplicity of `V(n)` in `M|_t` is `m_n = d_n − d_(n+2) ≥ 0`; conversely `d_i = Σ m_n` over `n ≥ |i|` with `n ≡ i (mod 2)`.

Prove `finrank_weightFiltration_eq_iff_exists_intertwiner`: for zero-inclusive triples `t` in `L` and `t'` in `L'` and finite-dimensional modules `M`, `M'`, the filtrations have equal dimensions in every degree if and only if some linear isomorphism `M ≅ M'` intertwines `ρ(h), ρ(e), ρ(f)` with `ρ'(h'), ρ'(e'), ρ'(f')`, that is, `M|_t ≅ M'|_(t')` (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1708).

A proof can proceed as follows. Split the `i`-th step as the `(i−1)`-th plus `M_i` using the internal direct sum `TauCeti.isInternal_eigenspace_toEnd_intCast`. In `V(n)` each weight `n, n−2, …, −n` has multiplicity one, so after decomposing by `TauCeti.Sl2Std.exists_isInternal_lieModuleEquiv` the number of summands `V(n)` is `d_n − d_(n+2)`; nonnegativity is the count of actual summands, not a property of natural-number subtraction. Equal cumulative dimensions give equal `d_i`, equal `m_n`, and isomorphic restrictions; the converse holds because an intertwiner preserves eigenspaces.

**Checks.**

- For `V(2) ⊕ 2V(0)`: `d_0 = 3`, `d_2 = d_(−2) = 1`, `m_0 = 2`, `m_2 = 1`, and the cumulative `dim W_0 = 4`.
- For `V(1) ⊕ V(0)`: `d_1 = 1`, `d_0 = 1`, `m_1 = d_1 − d_3 = 1`, `m_0 = d_0 − d_2 = 1`; computing `m_n` as `dim W_n − dim W_(n+2)` instead would give `m_0 = 2 − 3 < 0`, which is the error of reading cumulative dimensions as graded ones.
- The zero triple on `M` and the standard triple on the trivial module `K^(dim M)` of `sl₂` have equal filtrations (one jump at `0`), and the identity map intertwines.
- `V(2)` and `V(0) ⊕ V(1)` have dimension `3` but filtrations `(1, 1, 2, 2, 3)` and `(0, 1, 2, 3, 3)` at `i = −2, …, 2`, and no intertwiner.

*Needs:* §1.2; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.isInternal_eigenspace_toEnd_intCast`, `TauCeti.Sl2Std.exists_isInternal_lieModuleEquiv`, `TauCeti.finrank_eigenspace_toEnd_neg`; LieHighestWeight Layer 0.

### 1.4 Exactness, tensor products and duals

For a zero-inclusive triple, the assignment `M ↦ (M, W(M))` is exact and compatible with tensor products and duals. Every module map is strict: `φ(W_i M) = φ(M) ∩ W_i M'`. A short exact sequence `0 → M' → M → M'' → 0` stays exact on every step. `W_i(M ⊗ N) = Σ_(a+b=i) W_a M ⊗ W_b N`, equivalently the sum over `a + b ≤ i`. For the contragredient dual, `W_i(M*)` is the annihilator of `W_(−i−1)(M)`. The tensor unit has its only jump at `0`. The comparison maps are the identity on underlying tensors and the evaluation `M* ⊗ M → K`, so the associativity, symmetry, unit and evaluation constraints are respected (CH §2.2, Definition 2.2, p.6, CH line 288, where the filtration is a symmetric monoidal functor).

A proof can proceed as follows. A module map commutes with `ρ(h)` and so respects eigenspaces; injectivity and surjectivity are checked on each eigenspace, which gives strictness and exactness. On `M ⊗ N` eigenvalues add, and on `M*` they change sign (LieHighestWeight Layer 2); sum the eigenspaces. No claim that all filtered vector spaces form an abelian category is used.

**Checks.**

- `V(1) ⊗ V(1)` has graded multiplicities `1, 2, 1` at `−2, 0, 2`, so it is `V(2) ⊕ V(0)`; its cumulative dimensions are `1, 1, 3, 3, 4` at `i = −2, …, 2`.
- For `M = V(1)`, `W_(−1)(M*)` is the annihilator of `W_0(M) = K v_(−1)`, a line, and `W_0(M*)` is the annihilator of `W_(−1)(M) = K v_(−1)`; writing `W_(−i)` in place of `W_(−i−1)` would give the annihilator of `W_1(M) = M` at `i = −1`, the zero space, which is wrong.
- For the unit `K`, `W_i(K*) = Ann(W_(−i−1) K)` is `0` for `i < 0` and `K` for `i ≥ 0`, so duality fixes the filtration of the unit.

*Needs:* §1.2; §1.3; LieHighestWeight Layer 2.

### Examples

For the adjoint module of `sl(3, K)` along the minimal triple, `d = (1, 2, 2, 2, 1)` at `i = −2, …, 2`, so `m_2 = 1`, `m_1 = 2`, `m_0 = 1` and the cumulative dimensions are `1, 3, 5, 7, 8`; along the regular triple (`h = diag(2, 0, −2)`) the adjoint weights are `4, 2, 2, 0, 0, −2, −2, −4`, so `m_4 = 1`, `m_2 = 1`, and the cumulative dimensions differ at `i = −4`. By §1.3 and §0.3 these two triples are not conjugate, as their filtrations on the adjoint module already show.

## Layer 2: classical types

Throughout, `V` is finite-dimensional over `K`; in the orthogonal and symplectic cases `B` is a nondegenerate symmetric, respectively alternating, bilinear form on `V`, and the Lie algebra is `so(V, B)`, respectively `sp(V, B)`. The type `A` classification of nilpotent matrices by partitions is NilpotentOrbits N4; it is used here through §1.3 only.

### 2.1 Invariant forms on the standard sl₂-modules

On `V(n)` every `sl₂`-invariant bilinear form is a scalar multiple of one nonzero form `β_n`, and `β_n` is symmetric when `n` is even and alternating when `n` is odd. In the weight basis `v_n, v_(n−2), …, v_(−n)` with `ρ(f)v_k = v_(k−2)`, invariance forces `β_n(v_k, v_l) = 0` unless `k + l = 0`, and the recursion from `ρ(e)` determines the values `β_n(v_k, v_(−k))` up to one scalar, with `β_n(v_(−k), v_k) = (−1)^n β_n(v_k, v_(−k))`.

**Checks.**

- On `V(1) = K²` the invariant form is the determinant pairing, alternating.
- On `V(2)`, identified with the adjoint module, the invariant form is a multiple of the trace form `tr(xy)`, symmetric; its value on `(H, H)` is `2`.
- A form invariant under `H` alone is not determined up to scalar: on `V(2)` any form pairing `v_2, v_(−2)` and `v_0, v_0` with independent values is `H`-invariant, and only the `E, F` conditions tie these values together.

*Needs:* LieHighestWeight Layer 0.

### 2.2 Orthogonal and symplectic partitions

For `L = so(V, B)` or `sp(V, B)` and a zero-inclusive triple `t`, the restricted standard module decomposes as `⨁_(n ≥ 0) V(n) ⊗ U_n`, and `B` restricts to `β_n ⊗ γ_n` with `γ_n` a nondegenerate form on `U_n`, of the opposite symmetry type to `β_n` in the symplectic case and of the same type in the orthogonal case. Hence `e` has Jordan blocks of size `n + 1` with multiplicity `dim U_n`; in the orthogonal case every even block size has even multiplicity, in the symplectic case every odd block size has even multiplicity. Two triples of `so(V, B)` (respectively `sp(V, B)`) are conjugate under `O(V, B)(K)` (respectively `Sp(V, B)(K)`) if and only if their restrictions to `V` are isomorphic. An `O(V, B)`-class splits into two `SO(V, B)`-classes exactly when the partition is very even (every block size even, every multiplicity even); otherwise it is one `SO(V, B)`-class. (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1709, cites the partition classification; the proof here uses §2.1.)

A proof can proceed as follows. Distinct isotypic summands are `B`-orthogonal by Schur's lemma, and on `V(n) ⊗ U_n` the form factors through §2.1. Over `K` nondegenerate symmetric or alternating forms are classified by dimension, and alternating forms exist only in even dimension; this gives the parity conditions, existence, and uniqueness up to `O` or `Sp`. For the splitting, an element of the orthogonal centralizer acting by a reflection on a symmetric `U_n` has determinant `(−1)^(n+1)` on `V(n) ⊗ U_n`; a symmetric `U_n` with `n` even exists exactly when an odd block size occurs. If all block sizes are even, every element of the orthogonal centralizer has determinant `1`, and the index-two subgroup `SO` gives two classes.

**Checks.**

- In `sp(4)`, the partition `(3, 1)` is excluded (odd parts `3` and `1` each of multiplicity one) while `(2, 2)` occurs.
- In `so(8)`, `(4, 4)` and `(2, 2, 2, 2)` are very even and split into two `SO(8)`-classes; `(3, 3, 1, 1)` is not very even and is a single class.
- In `so(4) ≅ sl₂ × sl₂`, `(2, 2)` is very even: its two classes are the triples supported in one or the other factor, while `(3, 1)` is the diagonal triple.
- In `so(3) ≅ sl₂` the only partitions are `(3)` and `(1, 1, 1)`; `(2, 1)` is excluded because the even part `2` has odd multiplicity.

*Needs:* §1.1; §1.3; §2.1; LieHighestWeight Layers 0, 5; ReductiveGroups Layer 6.

### 2.3 Half-spin separation of very even classes

Let `L = so(2r)`, `r ≥ 2`, with the coordinates of the Conventions, and let `λ` be a very even partition of `2r`; then `r` is even. The neutral elements of the two `SO(2r)`-classes with partition `λ` are, in the dominant chamber, `h_I = (a₁, …, a_(r−1), a_r)` and `h_II = (a₁, …, a_(r−1), −a_r)` with odd `a₁ ≥ ⋯ ≥ a_r > 0` (the `r` positive `h`-eigenvalues on `V`, with multiplicity). On the half-spin module `L(ω_r)`, whose weights are the vectors `½(±1, …, ±1)` with an even number of minus signs, the largest index `i` with `W_i ≠ W_(i−1)` is `A = ½(a₁ + ⋯ + a_r)` for `h_I` and `A − a_r` for `h_II`. Hence the two classes have different filtrations on `L(ω_r)`. (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH lines 1716–1727.)

The ordering of coordinates matters. CH lists the simple roots as `±ε₁ + ε₂, ε₂ − ε₃, …` together with ascending coordinates `a₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ a_r`, which are not dominant for `ε₂ − ε₃` (for `(1, 1, 3, 3)` the pairing with `ε₂ − ε₃` is `−2`); with ascending coordinates the simple roots must be `±ε₁ + ε₂, ε₃ − ε₂, …, ε_r − ε_(r−1)`. The convention fixed above reverses the coordinates instead, and the maxima are unchanged.

A proof can proceed as follows. The value of `h` on the weight `½(s₁, …, s_r)` is `½ Σ s_i a_i`. For `h_I` the maximum over even sign vectors is at all signs `+`. For `h_II` the all-`+` vector gives `A − a_r`; the vector with one minus sign at `r` is odd and excluded; any even vector with a minus sign at some `i < r` and at `r` gives `A − a_i ≤ A − a_r`. Pass from different top indices to different filtrations by §1.3. The module `L(ω_r)` exists over `K` by LieHighestWeight Layer 4 and has the stated weights by Layer 6 (minuscule); over `ℂ` it is the even-sign half-spin module of SpinRepresentations Layer 5.

**Checks.**

- In `so(8)`, partition `(4, 4)`: the `h`-eigenvalues on `V` are `3, 3, 1, 1, −1, −1, −3, −3`, so `a = (3, 3, 1, 1)`, and the maxima on `L(ω₄)` are `4` and `3`.
- In `so(8)`, partition `(2, 2, 2, 2)`: `a = (1, 1, 1, 1)`, maxima `2` and `1`.
- On the standard module `V` both neutral elements have the same eigenvalues, so the standard filtrations agree; a test family consisting of `V` and its tensor powers cannot separate the classes, because the outer automorphism exchanging them fixes the class of `V`.
- Using the odd half-spin module `L(ω_(r−1))` exchanges the roles: the maxima become `A − a_r` for `h_I` and `A` for `h_II`; either half-spin module separates.

*Needs:* §1.3; §2.2; LieHighestWeight Layers 4, 6; RootSystems Layer 6; SpinRepresentations Layer 5.

### 2.4 Recognition in the classical types

Prove `exists_innerAut_of_finrank_weightFiltration_sl`, `exists_innerAut_of_finrank_weightFiltration_sp` and `exists_innerAut_of_finrank_weightFiltration_so`. For `L = sl(n, K)` acting on `Kⁿ`, `sp(2l, K)` acting on `K^(l ⊕ l)`, or `so(n, K)` acting on `Kⁿ`, two zero-inclusive triples whose filtrations on the standard module have equal dimensions in every degree are `Int(L)`-conjugate; for `so(n, K)` this is asserted when the neutral element has nonzero kernel on `Kⁿ`, which says the partition has a part of odd size and holds for every odd `n`. In type `D`, adding the half-spin module `L(ω_r)` to the standard module gives a test family that determines the class in all cases. (CH Appendix, Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH lines 1709–1727.)

A proof can proceed as follows. By §1.3 equal standard filtrations give isomorphic restrictions to the standard module, hence the same partition. In type `A` an intertwiner in `GL(V)` can be rescaled to determinant one since `K` is algebraically closed, and `Ad(SL_n(K)) = Int(sl_n)` by §0.2. In types `B, C, D` apply §2.2 to obtain conjugacy under `Sp` or `O`, and under `SO` away from very even partitions; then apply §0.2 for the connected groups `Sp` and `SO`. In the very even case apply §2.3. For `so(2)` (abelian) the only triple is the zero triple.

**Checks.**

- In `sl(3)` the minimal and regular triples have standard eigenvalues `(1, 0, −1)` and `(2, 0, −2)`, hence different standard filtrations; the zero triple has the single jump `3` at `0`.
- In `so(8)` with partition `(4, 4)`, the kernel of `h` on `K⁸` is zero, the two classes have equal standard filtrations and are not conjugate; the hypothesis on the kernel excludes exactly this case.
- In `so(7)` every triple has an odd part, and the standard module alone recognises triples.
- In `sp(2)` = `sl₂` acting on `K²`, the two classes have standard filtrations with jumps `2` at `0` and `1, 1` at `−1, 1`.

*Needs:* §0.2; §1.3; §2.2; §2.3; LieHighestWeight Layer 7 (Serre presentation identifying a simple algebra of classical type with its standard model); RootSystems Layer 5; ReductiveGroups Layer 6.

## Layer 3: exceptional types

`T` is one of `G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈`, of rank `r`, with root set `Φ_T ⊂ ℤ^r` in simple-root coordinates (RootSystems Layer 6).

### 3.1 Necessary conditions on a characteristic

Let `L` be simple with a fixed Cartan subalgebra `H` and base `{α_i}`, and `t` a zero-inclusive triple. Some `Int(L)`-conjugate of `h` lies in `H` and is dominant, and it is unique; its labels `a_i = α_i(h)` form the characteristic of `t`. The characteristic satisfies: each `a_i ∈ {0, 1, 2}`; `a` is fixed by the permutation of `−w₀`; and the adjoint graded multiplicities `d_m = dim L_m` satisfy `d_m = d_(−m)` and `d_m ≥ d_(m+2)` for every `m ≥ 0`.

A proof can proceed as follows. The neutral element is ad-semisimple with integer eigenvalues (Tau Ceti `TauCeti.iSup_eigenspace_toEnd_intCast_eq_top`); its centralizer is reductive of full rank (LieHighestWeight Layer 9), so it lies in a Cartan subalgebra, which is `Int(L)`-conjugate to `H` (LieHighestWeight Layer 7), and the Weyl group, realised in `Int(L)` by Weyl automorphisms, moves it into the dominant chamber, where it is unique (RootSystems Layer 4). The bound `a_i ≤ 2` is NilpotentOrbits N2.2. Since `h` is conjugate to `−h` (§0.2), and `−w₀` sends the dominant `−h`-representative back to the chamber, uniqueness gives `a = −w₀ a`. The inequalities are `m_m = d_m − d_(m+2) ≥ 0` for the adjoint module (§1.3). (SOM §3.1, p.15, SOM line 373; DG §2, p.3, DG line 86.)

**Checks.**

- For `E₆` the opposition condition is `a₁ = a₆` and `a₃ = a₅`, with `a₂, a₄` free; it removes all but `81` of the `729` label vectors.
- For `G₂`, the labels `(2, 0)` satisfy the label bound but fail the inequalities: their profile is `4, 0, 2, 0, 1, 0, 2` at `m = 0, …, 6`, and `d_4 = 1 < d_6 = 2`.
- The characteristic of the zero triple is `0`, and that of a principal triple is `(2, …, 2)`.

### 3.2 Adjoint root profiles

For `a ∈ ℤ^r` define the profile `D_T(a) : ℤ → ℕ` by `D_T(a)_m = r·[m = 0] + #{c ∈ Φ_T : Σ_i c_i a_i = m}`. If `a` is the characteristic of a triple in the simple algebra of type `T`, then `D_T(a)_m = dim ker(ad h − m)` for every `m`: each root space is a line on which `h` acts by `Σ c_i a_i`, and `H` is the zero-weight space of dimension `r` (LieHighestWeight Layer 1; DG §2, p.3, DG line 83).

API:

- `Σ_m D_T(a)_m = r + |Φ_T| = dim L`, the sum being finite.
- `D_T(a)_(−m) = D_T(a)_m`, by `c ↦ −c` on `Φ_T`.
- `D_T(a)_m = 0` for `|m| > B_T(a) = Σ_i a_i θ_i`, where `θ` is the highest root; for labels in `{0, 1, 2}` this bound is at most `2·ht(θ)`, that is `10, 22, 22, 34, 58`.
- For a characteristic, `D_T(a)` is the graded adjoint multiplicity function, and the cumulative adjoint filtration is `Σ_(j ≤ i) D_T(a)_j`.
- The root set `Φ_T` is the closure of the simple roots under the simple reflections `c ↦ c − (Σ_i c_i A_ij) α_j`, and has `12, 48, 72, 126, 240` elements.

**Checks.**

- At `a = 0`, `D_T(0)` is `dim L` (`14, 52, 78, 133, 248`) at `0` and zero elsewhere.
- For `G₂` at `a = (2, 2)` the values at `m = 0, …, 10` are `2, 0, 2, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1`, totalling `14` with the negative half.
- For `F₄` at `a = (2, 2, 2, 2)`, `D_0 = 4` and the values at `0, 2, 4, …, 22` are `4, 4, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 1, 1, 1`; the cumulative `Σ_(j ≤ 0) D_j` is `28`. Omitting the term `r·[m = 0]` gives total `48` instead of `52`.
- For `G₂` with `A = [[2, −1], [−3, 2]]`, `a = (0, 1)` (label on the long root `α₂`) gives `4, 4, 1` at `m = 0, 1, 2`, and `a = (1, 0)` gives `4, 2, 1, 2`; transposing the Cartan matrix without reindexing exchanges these two profiles.

*Needs:* §3.1; RootSystems Layer 6; LieHighestWeight Layer 1; Tau Ceti `TauCeti.DynkinType.cartanMatrix`.

### 3.3 Candidate characteristics and injectivity of profiles

Define the candidate set `C_T ⊂ {0, 1, 2}^r` as the set of `a` that are fixed by `−w₀` and satisfy `D_T(a)_m ≥ D_T(a)_(m+2)` for all `m ≥ 0`. By §3.1 every characteristic of a triple lies in `C_T`. Prove that `D_T` is injective on `C_T`, for each `T` separately: if `a, b ∈ C_T` and `D_T(a) = D_T(b)` then `a = b`. The statement is about the finite set `C_T` and asserts nothing about which candidates are characteristics. (CH Appendix, proof of Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1728, asserts the separation for the realised characteristics; the candidate set is a superset that makes the statement independent of the classification of orbits.)

API:

- `a ∈ C_T` if and only if every `a_i ∈ {0, 1, 2}`, `a` is fixed by `−w₀`, and `D_T(a)_m ≥ D_T(a)_(m+2)` for `0 ≤ m ≤ 2·ht(θ)`; beyond that range both sides vanish (§3.2), so the finitely many inequalities are equivalent to all of them.
- Soundness: the characteristic of every zero-inclusive triple of the simple algebra of type `T` lies in `C_T` (§3.1).
- The zero vector and the principal vector `(2, …, 2)` lie in `C_T`; they are the characteristics of the zero and the principal triples.

A proof can proceed as follows. Both conditions and the profile are integer computations on the finite sets `{0, 1, 2}^r` and `Φ_T`, with the profile supported in `[−58, 58]`; injectivity is a comparison of finitely many integer vectors. `E₆` and `E₇` are treated in their own root systems.

**Checks.**

- `|C_T|` is `7, 28, 34, 274, 245` for `G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈`, and the numbers of distinct profiles on `C_T` are the same.
- `C_(G₂)` consists of `(0, 0), (0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 0), (1, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)`, while `G₂` has five nilpotent orbits; `(1, 1)` and `(1, 2)` are candidates that are not characteristics. Membership in `C_T` is a necessary condition only.
- Without the opposition condition, `E₆` has `212` label vectors satisfying the inequalities and only `123` distinct profiles: `a = (1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)` and `(0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1)` have the same profile. The opposition condition is what makes the profile injective.
- The zero vector lies in every `C_T`, with the profile of the zero triple.
- The numbers of nilpotent orbits, the zero orbit included, are `5, 16, 21, 45, 70` (DG Appendix A, Tables 2–6, pp.7–16); each `C_T` is strictly larger than the set of characteristics, and §3.4 uses only injectivity on `C_T`, not a list of orbits.

*Needs:* §3.1; §3.2.

### 3.4 Exceptional adjoint separation

Prove `exists_innerAut_of_finrank_weightFiltration_adjoint_exceptional`: if `L` is isomorphic to `LieAlgebra.g₂ K`, `LieAlgebra.f₄ K`, `LieAlgebra.e₆ K`, `LieAlgebra.e₇ K` or `LieAlgebra.e₈ K` (and is finite-dimensional Killing), two zero-inclusive triples whose filtrations on the adjoint module have equal dimensions in every degree are `Int(L)`-conjugate. The adjoint module alone is a test family in every exceptional type, `E₆` and `E₇` included (CH Appendix, Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1728).

A proof can proceed as follows. Equal cumulative adjoint dimensions give equal graded multiplicities, which by §3.2 are the profiles of the two characteristics. Both characteristics lie in `C_T` (§3.1), so §3.3 makes them equal; the dominant neutral elements in `H` are then equal, because the simple roots form a basis of `H*`, and §0.3 gives conjugacy of the triples. The identification of `L` with a split algebra with root system of type `T` is LieHighestWeight Layer 7.

The `E₆` and `E₇` cases are proved in their own root systems: restricting from `E₈` to a Levi subalgebra does not yield them, since equality of the filtration on the Levi summand of the `E₈` adjoint module does not control the other summands, and conjugacy in `E₈` need not be conjugacy in the Levi subalgebra.

**Checks.**

- The theorem covers the zero triple, whose adjoint filtration has the single jump `dim L` at `0`.
- In `G₂` the five classes have profiles (nonnegative half) `14`; `4, 4, 1`; `4, 2, 1, 2`; `4, 0, 4, 0, 1`; `2, 0, 2, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1`, at labels `0, (0, 1), (1, 0), (0, 2), (2, 2)`; they are pairwise distinct.
- In type `D` the adjoint module does not separate: the two very even classes of `so(8)` with partition `(4, 4)` are exchanged by an automorphism, which fixes the class of the adjoint module, so their adjoint filtrations are equal. This is why Layer 2 uses the standard and half-spin modules; the exceptional statement is specific to the five types.

*Needs:* §0.3; §1.3; §3.1; §3.2; §3.3; LieHighestWeight Layer 7; RootSystems Layer 5.

## Layer 4: semisimple recognition

### 4.1 Filtration recognition

Prove `exists_innerAut_iff_finrank_weightFiltration`: for a finite-dimensional semisimple `L` over `K` and zero-inclusive triples `t, t'`, the following are equivalent: (1) `t` and `t'` are `Int(L)`-conjugate; (2) for every finite-dimensional `L`-module `M` and every `i ∈ ℤ`, `dim W_i^t(M) = dim W_i^(t')(M)`; (3) for every such `M` the restrictions `M|_t` and `M|_(t')` are isomorphic. In (2) it suffices to test the pullbacks, along the projections to the simple ideals, of the standard module of each classical factor, one half-spin module of each factor of type `D`, and the adjoint module of each exceptional factor. (CH Lemma 2.4, p.6, CH line 296; CH Appendix, Lemma 10.9, p.35, CH line 1701.)

A proof can proceed as follows. (1) ⇒ (2) is inner invariance (§0.2); (2) ⇔ (3) is §1.3. For (2) ⇒ (1), decompose `L = L₁ ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ L_k` into simple ideals (LieHighestWeight Layer 5), so that `Int(L) = Int(L₁) × ⋯ × Int(L_k)` and each triple decomposes componentwise. Pull back a module of one factor along its projection: the other ideals act by zero, and the hypothesis gives that factor's recognition hypothesis, classical (§2.4) or exceptional (§3.4), using the type decomposition of RootSystems Layer 5. The product of the factor conjugators lies in `Int(L)`. Pullback modules are factor-selective, so isomorphic simple factors are never exchanged. The case `L = 0` has only the zero triple.

**Checks.**

- For `L = sl₂ × sl₂`, the triples `(t, 0)` and `(0, t)` are exchanged by the swap automorphism, which is outer; the pullback of the standard module of the first factor separates them (filtration jumps `1, 1` at `±1` versus `2` at `0`).
- For `L = so(8)` the half-spin test is legitimate even when the group in view is `SO(8)` or its adjoint form: the quantifier is over `L`-modules.
- For `L = 0`, `Int(L)` is trivial, the only triple is zero, and every module test holds trivially.
- For `L = sl₂ × sl₂` the adjoint module of `L` does not separate `(t, 0)` from `(0, t)`: both have adjoint weights `2, 0, −2, 0, 0, 0`. The test family must consist of factor-selective pullbacks, not of the adjoint module of `L`.

*Needs:* §0.2; §1.3; §2.4; §3.4; LieHighestWeight Layer 5; RootSystems Layer 5.

### Examples

For `L = sl(3)`, the three classes of triples (zero, minimal, regular) have standard filtration jumps `3` at `0`; `1, 1, 1` at `−1, 0, 1`; and `1, 1, 1` at `−2, 0, 2`, so the standard module alone separates them, as §2.4 predicts.

## Layer 5: highest weights and weight patterns

### 5.1 Half-integrality on a root-lattice coset

Let `X` be an abelian group, `Q ⊆ X` the subgroup generated by finitely many elements `α_i`, `ν : X → ℝ` additive and `λ ∈ X`. If `ν(α_i) ∈ ½ℤ` for every `i`, then for every `μ ∈ λ + Q`, `ν(μ) ∈ ½ℤ` if and only if `ν(λ) ∈ ½ℤ`. More generally the statement holds with any additive subgroup of `ℝ` in place of `½ℤ`. Here `ν(x) ∈ ½ℤ` means `2ν(x) ∈ ℤ`. (CH §6.12, proof of Lemma 6.30, p.24, CH line 1163.)

**Checks.**

- For `A₁` with `α = 2ω` and `ν(α) = ½`, `ν(ω) = ¼`: the weights `±ω` of the standard module have `ν = ±¼ ∉ ½ℤ`, matching `ν(ω) ∉ ½ℤ`; the adjoint weights `α, 0, −α` have values in `½ℤ`, matching `ν(α) ∈ ½ℤ`.
- Without the hypothesis on the `α_i` the statement fails: with `ν(α) = ⅓`, `λ = 0` and `μ = α`, `ν(λ) ∈ ½ℤ` but `ν(μ) ∉ ½ℤ`.
- With `Q = 0` the coset is `{λ}` and the statement is trivial.

*Needs:* none beyond additive groups.

### 5.2 The highest-weight half-integrality test

Let `L` be finite-dimensional semisimple over `K` with a Cartan subalgebra, a base `{α_i}` and real weight space `X ⊗ ℝ`, and let `ν` be a real linear functional with `ν(α_i) ∈ ½ℤ` for every simple root. For every dominant integral `λ`, all weights `μ` of `L(λ)` satisfy `ν(μ) ∈ ½ℤ` if and only if `ν(λ) ∈ ½ℤ`. The same holds for an irreducible algebraic representation of a connected semisimple group over `K` with highest weight `λ` in the actual character lattice; no dominance of `ν` is assumed. (CH §6.12, Lemma 6.30, p.24, CH line 1158.)

A proof can proceed as follows. Every weight of `L(λ)` lies in `λ − Σ ℕα_i` and `λ` occurs (LieHighestWeight Layer 3); apply §5.1 in one direction and use the occurrence of `λ` in the other. For an irreducible algebraic representation the weights are those of its differential with respect to the Lie algebra of a maximal torus, and the differential is irreducible in characteristic zero (ReductiveGroups Layers 2, 10B); apply the Lie statement.

**Checks.**

- For `G = PGL₂` the character lattice is `ℤα`; with `ν(α) = ½` every algebraic highest weight `λ = kα` has `ν(λ) = k/2 ∈ ½ℤ`, so every weight of every algebraic representation of `PGL₂` is half-integral, whereas the Lie module `L(ω)` of `sl₂`, which is not algebraic for `PGL₂`, has `ν(ω) = ¼`.
- For `L = sl₂`, `λ = 3ω` and `ν(α) = ½`: `ν(λ) = ¾ ∉ ½ℤ`, and indeed `ν(ω) = ¼ ∉ ½ℤ` for the weight `ω`.
- Strengthening the hypothesis to `ν(ω_i) ∈ ½ℤ` for all fundamental weights would make both sides of the equivalence always true; it is not assumed.

*Needs:* §5.1; LieHighestWeight Layers 2, 3, 4; ReductiveGroups Layers 2, 10B.

### 5.3 Weight patterns

Fix `q > 1`. For a finite-dimensional complex space `V` and a semisimple `A ∈ GL(V)` all of whose eigenvalues have absolute value in `q^(ℤ/2)`, define `n_A : ℤ → ℕ` by letting `n_A(i)` be the sum of the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of absolute value `q^(i/2)`. For a `q`-geometric semisimple `s` in a connected complex semisimple group `G`, its weight pattern is the function `(τ, i) ↦ n_(τ(s))(i)` on pairs of a finite-dimensional algebraic representation `τ` and an integer `i` (CH §10.1, p.32, CH line 1550).

API:

- `n_A(i)` is the sum of the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of absolute value `q^(i/2)`; since `i ↦ q^(i/2)` is injective, each eigenvalue is counted for exactly one `i`, and `Σ_i n_A(i) = dim V`.
- Patterns add under direct sums; the tensor unit has `n(0) = 1` and `n(i) = 0` otherwise.
- `n_(A⊗B)(i) = Σ_(j+k=i) n_A(j) n_B(k)`; `n_(A∨)(i) = n_A(−i)` for the contragredient.
- Similar operators, and conjugate group elements, have equal patterns; isomorphic representations give equal patterns.
- If `s` is `q`-geometric in one faithful representation it is `q`-geometric in every representation, because every algebraic representation of a semisimple group is a subquotient of tensor products of a faithful one and its dual.

**Checks.**

- The zero space gives the zero function; the one-dimensional trivial representation gives the indicator of `0`.
- In `SL₂`, `diag(q^(1/2), q^(−1/2))` has pattern `1` at `±1` in the standard representation, and its tensor square has `1, 2, 1` at `2, 0, −2`.
- In `SL₂`, `diag(i, −i)` and the identity have the same pattern in every algebraic representation (all absolute values are `1`) but are not conjugate; the pattern forgets phases.

*Needs:* ReductiveGroups Layers 1, 4.

### 5.4 Positive real part

For a complex torus `T` with character lattice `X`, every `s ∈ T(ℂ) = Hom(X, ℂ×)` factors uniquely as `s = r(s)u(s)` with `χ(r(s)) = |χ(s)| ∈ ℝ_(>0)` and `|χ(u(s))| = 1` for every `χ ∈ X`. For a semisimple `s` in a connected complex semisimple group `G`, choose a maximal torus containing `s` and define the positive real part `r(s)` there; its `G(ℂ)`-conjugacy class does not depend on the choice, and after moving `s` into a fixed maximal torus `r(s)` is well defined up to the Weyl group (CH §10.1, proof of Proposition 10.2, p.32, CH line 1555).

A proof can proceed as follows. Absolute value is multiplicative, so `χ ↦ |χ(s)|` is a homomorphism `X → ℝ_(>0) ⊂ ℂ×`, a point of `T(ℂ)`; no logarithm or basis of `X` is chosen. Two maximal tori containing `s` are maximal tori of the connected group `Z_G(s)°` and are conjugate by an element fixing `s`, which transports `r(s)`. The Weyl group commutes with `χ ↦ |χ(·)|`. In an algebraic representation the eigenvalues of `τ(r(s))` are the absolute values of those of `τ(s)`, with the same multiplicities.

API:

- `χ(r(s)) = |χ(s)|` for every `χ ∈ X`, and this determines `r(s)`.
- `r(st) = r(s)r(t)` and `r(1) = 1` inside a fixed torus.
- `r(w·s) = w·r(s)` for `w` in the Weyl group.
- The multiset of eigenvalues of `τ(r(s))` is the multiset of absolute values of the eigenvalues of `τ(s)`; hence `tr τ(r(s)) = Σ_i n_(τ(s))(i) q^(i/2)` for `q`-geometric `s`.

**Checks.**

- If every character value has absolute value `1`, then `r(s) = 1`; compact phases are discarded.
- In `SL₂`, `r(diag(z, z⁻¹)) = diag(|z|, |z|⁻¹)`; the Weyl involution replaces `|z|` by `|z|⁻¹`, and both represent one conjugacy class.
- For `z = i`, `r(s)` is the identity although `s = diag(i, −i)` is not.
- For `PGL₂` the character lattice is `ℤα`; `r` is computed from `|α(s)|` only, and `diag(z, 1)` and `diag(−z, 1)` have the same positive part.

*Needs:* ReductiveGroups Layers 1, 4, 7.

### 5.5 Recognition of the positive real part

Fix `q > 1`. If semisimple `s, s'` in a connected complex semisimple group `G` are `q`-geometric and have equal weight patterns, then `r(s)` and `r(s')` are `G(ℂ)`-conjugate, equivalently Weyl-conjugate after moving them into a fixed maximal torus. It suffices to compare patterns on irreducible representations. No conjugacy of `s` and `s'` themselves is asserted. (CH §10.1, Proposition 10.2, p.32, CH line 1554.)

A proof can proceed as follows. For each `τ`, `tr τ(r(s)) = Σ_i n_(τ(s))(i) q^(i/2)` by §5.4, so equal patterns give equal traces at `r(s)` and `r(s')` in every algebraic representation; direct-sum additivity reduces to irreducibles. Both are semisimple, so ReductiveGroupsPartIII Layer RG3.0 (characters separate semisimple classes) gives conjugacy.

**Checks.**

- `diag(i, −i)` and `1` in `SL₂` have equal patterns; the conclusion `r = 1` for both holds, and the elements are not conjugate.
- `diag(q^(1/2), q^(−1/2))` and `diag(q^(−1/2), q^(1/2))` have equal patterns and conjugate positive parts (Weyl involution).
- `diag(q^(1/2), q^(−1/2))` and the identity have different patterns in the standard representation (`1` at `±1` versus `2` at `0`), and their positive parts are not conjugate.

*Needs:* §5.3; §5.4; ReductiveGroupsPartIII Layer RG3.0; ReductiveGroups Layers 7, 10B.

### Examples

For `G = SL₂` and `s = diag(q^(1/2)·e^(iθ), q^(−1/2)·e^(−iθ))`, the pattern in `Sym^k` of the standard representation is `1` at `k, k−2, …, −k`, independent of `θ`, and `r(s) = diag(q^(1/2), q^(−1/2))`, which is `exp((½ log q) H)` for the standard neutral element `H`.

### Dependencies

ReductiveGroups Layers 1, 2, 4, 7, 10B; ReductiveGroupsPartIII Layer RG3.0; LieHighestWeight Layers 2, 3, 4.

## Downstream consumers

The recognition theorems of Layers 1–4 and the positive-real-part recognition of Layer 5 are the representation-theoretic inputs of the Ciubotaru–Harris argument for the generalized Ramanujan and Arthur conjectures over function fields: Lemma 2.4 compares Frobenius weight filtrations of Langlands parameters, and Proposition 10.2 identifies the real part of a Satake parameter from its weight patterns. Any consumer comparing representation-indexed filtrations or weight data of parameters for a complex reductive dual group uses these interfaces; none of them feeds back into this roadmap.
